Satellites orbit the earth in circular or elliptical orbits and at different altitudes. Since 1961, these have included amateur radio satellites. They are called OSCAR, which is the abbreviation of "Orbiting Satellite Carrying Amateur Radio". The first amateur radio satellite was named OSCAR 1 ([ref:n_satellit_oscar1]). OSCAR 1 was only the beginning. In the years that followed – up to today – a whole series of ever more extensively equipped amateur radio payloads has been launched into space.

[question:BE415]

The repeaters carried on board are called "transponders". The input frequency, i.e. the radio path from the earth to the satellite, is called the "uplink" in satellite communication. The output frequency, i.e. the radio path from the satellite to the earth, on the other hand, is called the "downlink". Different frequency bands are often used for the uplink and the downlink, because this makes it easier to separate the transmitted and received signals and reduces the size of the filters on the satellite.

<webindepth>
Different altitudes and orbits allow satellites to be used for a wide range of applications, from communication through navigation to scientific research and earth observation. Below we present the most important altitudes and orbits.

*Low Earth Orbit (LEO)*
Satellites in low orbits are usually positioned at altitudes of about 160 to 2,000 kilometres above the earth. These are orbits relatively close to the earth's surface. Many earth observation satellites move in this region, such as weather satellites or environmental monitoring satellites. Their closeness to the earth allows high resolution when capturing data and images.

*Medium Earth Orbit (MEO)*
Medium orbits extend over altitudes of about 2,000 to 35,786 kilometres above the earth. This region is often home to navigation satellites, such as those used for the world-famous GPS system. Since the satellites here take longer to orbit the earth, they offer a good balance between coverage and accuracy for navigation and positioning.

*High orbits (Geostationary Orbit, GEO)*
The geostationary orbits are at an altitude of about 35,786 kilometres above the earth's surface. These satellites orbit the earth at the same speed at which the earth rotates, so that they appear to stand still in the sky. Geostationary satellites are ideal for communication purposes, since they can use fixed receiving stations and thus provide constant coverage of a particular area.

*Highly Elliptical Orbit (HEO)*
Highly elliptical orbits are eccentric orbits in which satellites have their smallest distance from the earth in one region and their greatest distance in another. This type of orbit is well suited to satellites that need extended coverage of the polar regions, for example for monitoring tasks in Arctic or Antarctic areas.

*Polar orbits*
Satellites operating in polar orbits fly over the earth's poles. Over time, these orbits provide complete coverage of the earth's surface, and they are often used for scientific investigations, environmental monitoring and earth observation.
</webindepth>

In satellite communication, the alignment of the antennas is of central importance. The terms *azimuth* and *elevation* play a key role here. They describe the horizontal direction and the vertical angle at which a satellite is seen from the earth's surface:
* The azimuth is the direction along the horizon in which you look to see the satellite. It is usually measured in degrees and runs from $\qty{0}{\degree}$ (north) through $\qty{90}{\degree}$ (east) and $\qty{180}{\degree}$ (south) to $\qty{270}{\degree}$ (west).
* The elevation is the vertical angle at which a satellite stands above the horizon, seen from your position. It is also measured in degrees and varies from $\qty{0}{\degree}$ (right on the horizon) to $\qty{90}{\degree}$ (directly overhead).

In the amateur-satellite service there is an exception to the obligation to use only plain language. Control signals between earth command stations and amateur satellites may be encoded for the purpose of obscuring their meaning. This means that, by way of exception, encryption methods may be used for them which prevent third parties from reading the content of the control signals. This protects the satellites from control commands sent by unauthorised persons.

[question:VA303]

Under German law – but not internationally – this rule also applies to the control signals for automatic and remotely controlled stations and for remote stations.
