In amateur radio we often deal with both very large and very small numbers. We have already met the unit of frequency – the hertz ($\unit{\hertz}$) – and the typical prefixes for large numbers such as *kilo* ($\unit{\kilo}$), *mega* ($\unit{\mega}$) and *giga* ($\unit{\giga}$).

There are such unit prefixes for small numbers too. A well-known one, for example, is *milli*, abbreviated $\unit{\milli}$. One millimetre is one thousandth of a metre, i.e. $\qty{0.001}{\meter}$. The most commonly used prefixes for large and small numbers are listed in Table [ref:e_einheitenvorzeichen].

Besides the units hertz and metre, which we have already met, there are many other physical units that we will deal with later in the course. They include, for example, electrical voltage, measured in volts ($\unit{\volt}$), electric current in amperes ($\unit{\ampere}$), power in watts ($\unit{\watt}$), electrical resistance in ohms ($\unit{\ohm}$) and others.

To understand what follows, you do not yet need to know these units in detail. For now, all that matters is to understand what the unit prefixes mean and how they are used.

Unfortunately, these unit prefixes cannot be entered directly into every calculator. Instead, what are called powers of ten are used.

---

Let us first look at this using the example of the prefix kilo, which stands for $1000$: instead of $1000$ you can also write $10 \cdot 10 \cdot 10$. The short form for this is $10^3$ (spoken "10 to the power of 3" or "the 3rd power of 10"). The values $\qty{1500}{\hertz}$, $\qty{1.5}{\kilo\hertz}$ and $\qty{1.5e3}{\hertz}$ all stand for the same value.

<tip>
You can also remember it another way: you multiply the number by 10 as many times as the superscript number (the exponent) says. For numbers without a decimal point, you simply add as many zeros as the exponent says.
</tip>

For one million you have to multiply not three but six copies of the number 10 together. So the prefix mega corresponds to the value $10^6$. The notation $\qty{28e6}{\hertz}$ therefore means the same as $\qty{28}{\mega\hertz}$.

It works in a similar way for small numbers. One thousandth is $\frac{1}{10} \cdot \frac{1}{10} \cdot \frac{1}{10}$. This is written as $10^{-3}$ ("ten to the power of minus three"). So the value $\qty{3.5e-3}{\volt}$ is nothing other than $\qty{3.5}{\milli\volt}$, and $\qty{22e-6}{\volt}$ stands for $\qty{22}{\micro\volt}$. The most important powers of ten are again listed in Table [ref:e_einheitenvorzeichen].

---

Scientific calculators usually have a key for powers, mostly labelled $x^y$. Some models also offer a simpler way of entering powers of ten: a key labelled *Exp*, *E* or $\cdot 10^x$. It stands for "times ten to the power of …" and makes it easier to enter very large or very small numbers.

Example: to enter the value $\num{3.5e6}$, you press: 3.5 → Exp → 6

<tip>
It is best to look in your calculator's instruction manual to find the exact key sequence.
</tip>

How particularly large or small values are displayed depends on the device. Some write them as ordinary powers of ten, others with *Exp* or *E* for *times 10 to the power of*. The *S/D* key often switches between different representations. In power-of-ten notation, keys such as *ENG*, *<ENG* or *ENG>* move the decimal point or add zeros at the end, adjusting the exponent so that the value does not change. Here too, it is best to look in your calculator's instruction manual.
