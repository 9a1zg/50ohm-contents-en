We have already dealt with the basics of the ionosphere, sunspot activity and the "skip zone" in the exam questions for Class N. In Class E these topics are now taken further and extended by additional aspects of propagation.

Radiation from the sun knocks electrons out of the oxygen and nitrogen atoms and molecules of the upper atmosphere (*ionisation*). These free electrons are set oscillating by radio waves of a suitable frequency; this ultimately causes these waves to be bent back towards the earth's surface. As can be seen in Figure [ref:e_wellenausbreitung_refraktion], the change in direction of the radio waves caused by refraction – that is, bending – gives the impression that they are being reflected.

[question:EH101]

It is this process above all that gives HF its unique capabilities. The density of these free electrons determines which frequencies can still be refracted: the higher the density, the higher the frequency. We therefore need high ionisation for the upper HF bands, and so a high level of the solar radiation needed for it. That is why the $\qty{10}{\meter}$ band ($\qtyrange{28.0}{29.7}{\mega\hertz}$) works particularly well during the day, especially at sunspot maximum – even with low transmit power.

On the other hand, these free electrons tend to recombine straight away with the positive ions to form neutral atoms (*recombination*), and so lose their refracting effect. Since in sunlight ionisation and recombination go on continuously, an equilibrium between the two processes is established at certain heights. These areas are called *regions* (sometimes also "layers"). 

---

Three regions of the ionosphere are particularly important for the HF range: the D, E and F regions, as shown in Figures [ref:e_wellenausbreitung_refraktion] and [ref:e_schichten_jahreszeiten]. For the Class E exam questions, only a basic, qualitative understanding of these regions is needed. You should know that the F regions of the ionosphere, which are particularly important for long-distance HF propagation (DX), are at a height of about 130 to 450 km. Only in Class A do you also need to be able to state the exact heights of the individual regions by day and by night.

[question:EH102]

<margin>

</margin>

**D region**

The lowest of the three is the *D region*, at a height of about $\num{50}$ to $\qty{90}{\kilo\meter}$. Here, however, the gas density is still so high that the electrons set oscillating quickly collide with other particles. As a result, the radio wave loses energy. The D region therefore *attenuates* these waves, even to the point of extinguishing them completely. This effect becomes weaker as the frequency rises and is no longer significant above about $\qty{10}{\mega\hertz}$.

When radiation no longer arrives from the sun after sunset, the electrons recombine immediately and the D region practically disappears. That is why the lower HF bands, e.g. $\qty{80}{\meter}$ or $\qty{160}{\meter}$ (just like long and medium wave), are largely empty during the day: radio waves can then propagate only by the ground wave, because the sky wave is more or less swallowed up by the D region.

[question:EH105]

---

**E region**

Next, at a height of $\num{90}$ to $\qty{130}{\kilo\meter}$, comes the *E region*, which we already met in the exam questions for Class N. Under normal conditions it refracts radio waves arriving at an oblique angle up to about $\qty{10}{\mega\hertz}$, and a little more than $\qty{2000}{\kilo\meter}$ can be covered in one hop. The E region, too, disappears within a few minutes after sunset.

In this region a special phenomenon is found in the temperate latitudes, mainly in the summer months: the *sporadic E layer* (*Es* for short). It consists of mostly small, very strongly ionised areas and is able to refract radio waves of much higher frequency, up into the $\qty{2}{\meter}$ band.

---

**F region**

Finally, above that, is the *F region*, which during the day also splits into the F1 region and the F2 region above it. The latter can extend up to a height of $\qty{450}{\kilo\meter}$ and is the most significant for HF propagation. For one thing, its greater height allows distances of up to $\qty{4000}{\kilo\meter}$ to be covered in one hop. For another, the low gas density at these heights means that it takes a very long time for free electrons to find an ion again with which to recombine into a neutral atom or molecule. That is why there are always enough free electrons there to refract HF waves. Their number, and so their density, decreases, however, as solar radiation declines, and with it the highest frequency that is still bent back. That is why the upper bands close first.

[question:EH103]

Since the E region no longer exists at night, all long-distance radio traffic then goes via the F region.

[question:EH104]

---

Ionisation requires radiation in the extreme ultraviolet and X-ray ranges. The intensity of this radiation, which is produced by the sun, follows several cycles. The most important of them — as we have already learnt in the exam questions for Class N — is the solar cycle of about eleven years (see Figure [ref:e_ionosphaere_sonnenflecken]). 

<webmargin>
[picture:729:e_ionosphaere_sonnenflecken:The number of sunspots, which varies over the eleven-year solar cycle]
</webmargin>

[question:EH107]

During the activity maximum, solar activity reaches a particularly high level and leads to pronounced ionisation of the F region. That is why all the upper HF bands, and at times even the $\qty{6}{\meter}$ band, can then be used for DX contacts, whereas at the activity minimum the bands above the $\qty{20}{\meter}$ band (e.g. $\qty{10}{\meter}$) are mostly not usable.
