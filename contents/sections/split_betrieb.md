A special technique often used by sought-after stations is split operation. In split operation, the sought-after station receives on a different frequency from the one on which it transmits. The station calling CQ announces split operation by giving the frequency or the frequency range on which it receives. The figures are always given in $\unit{\kilo\hertz}$.

<indepth>
One *advantage of split operation* is that the special station can still be received clearly and does not itself get "lost" in the pile-up.
</indepth>

---

The receive frequency can be given as an offset from the transmit frequency. For example, "split 5 up" means that the calling station is listening 5 kHz above its transmit frequency. If, for example, the station transmits on $\qty{28410}{\kilo\hertz}$, as shown in Figure [ref:n_split_verkehr_anruffrequenz], you would have to reply on $\qty{28415}{\kilo\hertz}$.

More often, however, a sought-after station gives not just a single frequency but a whole frequency range. A station calling on $\qty{28410}{\kilo\hertz}$ means by "split up 28420 to 28430", or in short "tuning 420 to 430 up", that it receives on constantly changing frequencies in the range from $\qty{28420}{\kilo\hertz}$ to $\qty{28430}{\kilo\hertz}$ (Figure [ref:n_split_verkehr_anrufbereich]).

<margin>
[picture:673:n_split_verkehr_anrufbereich:Split operation, transmit frequency of the sought-after station and the frequency range for replies in which it receives]
</margin>

<indepth>
The *trick with the frequency range* in split operation is that nobody knows which frequency the sought-after station is listening on at any given moment. So you have to pick a frequency from this range at random and reply there. For the station calling CQ, the advantage is that it can pick out individual call signs more easily, since only a small proportion of the interested radio amateurs hit the right frequency.
</indepth>
