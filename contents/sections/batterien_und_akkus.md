As we have already learnt, batteries supply an electric voltage because charges are separated inside them. This is achieved by electrochemical processes, which take place as soon as the circuit is closed. Accumulators, or rechargeable batteries as they are usually called, work in much the same way. Their special feature, however, is that they can be recharged. To do this, a voltage is applied to the battery and the electrochemical reaction runs in reverse. After that, discharging can begin again. Batteries, on the other hand, cannot be recharged and can only be used once.

Handheld transceivers mostly use rechargeable batteries, but sometimes non-rechargeable batteries. To operate radio stations independently of the mains, e.g. on a Field Day, rechargeable batteries are often used.

The printing on batteries (Figure [ref:n_Bat_AA]) marks, for example, the positive and negative terminals and points out that they must be inserted with the correct polarity. With batteries, the warning "Not rechargeable" must always be observed.

Figure [ref:n_schaltzeichen_batt] shows the circuit symbol of a battery or rechargeable battery. The long line in the circuit symbol marks the positive terminal, the short one the negative terminal. A useful mnemonic: a plus sign needs 2 lines, but a minus sign only one.

There is a wide variety of batteries and rechargeable batteries with different voltages, capacities and types:
* Common voltages for batteries or rechargeable batteries are, for example, $\qty{1.5}{\volt}$ or $\qty{9}{\volt}$. But they also come with other voltages. Radio-controlled model cars, for example, mostly use $\qty{7.2}{\volt}$. Battery-powered tools often have rechargeable batteries of $\qty{18}{\volt}$, $\qty{20}{\volt}$ or $\qty{40}{\volt}$.
* The capacity of a battery or rechargeable battery is given in ampere-hours ($\unit{\ampere\hour}$). If a rechargeable battery has a capacity of $\qty{5}{\ampere\hour}$, it can supply a current of one ampere for 5 hours – or, for example, a current of 0.5 amperes for 10 hours, or a current of $\qty{5}{\ampere}$ for only one hour. With batteries, the capacity is often not stated. Ordinary household batteries often have a capacity of less than $\qty{5}{\ampere\hour}$. Large rechargeable batteries can have a capacity of $\qty{100}{\ampere\hour}$ or more. Unlike batteries, rechargeable batteries practically always have their capacity stated.
* Among the types, the cylindrical AA and AAA cells, which are used in most household devices, are very well known. But rechargeable batteries in particular come in all kinds of shapes – often even ones that fit only a single device.

<attention>
Rechargeable batteries should never be discharged completely. This so-called deep discharge can damage the battery. In practice, discharging can be recognised by the battery voltage gradually falling slightly. Current must stop being drawn before the voltage falls below the minimum voltage stated by the manufacturer.
</attention>

Many devices need several batteries. As a rule, this is to increase the voltage when the voltage of a single battery, e.g. $\qty{1.5}{\volt}$, is not enough to operate the device. In the battery compartment of the device they are connected in series, so that the negative terminal of each battery meets the positive terminal of the next. The voltage at the ends of this chain is calculated as follows:

$\text{Total voltage} = \text{number of batteries} \cdot \text{battery voltage}$

In general, a short circuit should be prevented with batteries and rechargeable batteries. With powerful modern rechargeable batteries in particular, there is a risk of overheating. They can catch fire, or cause a fire through the resulting short-circuit current.

<danger>
While in power supply units a fuse can stop the flow of current in the event of a fault, batteries and rechargeable batteries mostly lack this protection mechanism. The current that batteries and rechargeable batteries can deliver is often many times the maximum current of power supply units. This applies in particular to high-capacity rechargeable batteries such as car batteries, which can deliver $\qty{1000}{\ampere}$ or more for a short time. Rechargeable batteries use a wide variety of technologies based on different electrochemical reactions: lead-acid batteries have been used in cars for many decades. Small portable devices used to use nickel-cadmium (NiCd) batteries and later nickel-metal hydride (NiMH) technology. In mobile phones, digital cameras and notebook computers, lithium-ion batteries dominate today. In amateur radio, lithium iron phosphate compounds (LiFePO4) are increasingly being used too.

The differences in the electrochemical reactions have to be taken into account when charging these different types of rechargeable battery. In each case, chargers specially adapted to the technology must be used. Improper charging and discharging can cause the batteries to overheat. Touching them can then cause dangerous burns. Overheating can also make the batteries explode or cause fires. Liquids that escape can cause chemical burns or poisoning.

<attention>
Batteries and rechargeable batteries must always be disposed of properly. They do not belong in household waste! This is indicated by the crossed-out wheelie bin symbol (compare Figure [ref:n_Bat_AA]).
</attention>
