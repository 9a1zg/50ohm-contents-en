In Class E we have already met converters and transverters, which are used in amateur radio to open up additional frequency ranges with existing radios that these radios do not originally cover. As shown in Figure [ref:a_konverter_2], this requires an oscillator, a mixer and a band-pass filter.  

[question:AF301]

One problem here, which we now want to look at more closely, is that the amateur bands differ in width. The $\qty{70}{\centi\meter}$ band, for example, from $\qtyrange{430}{440}{\mega\hertz}$ with a width of $\qty{10}{\mega\hertz}$, is considerably wider than the $\qty{10}{\meter}$ band from $\qtyrange{28}{29.7}{\mega\hertz}$ with a width of $\qty{1.7}{\mega\hertz}$. As a result, a converter that converts the frequency range $\qtyrange{430}{440}{\mega\hertz}$ to the frequency range $\qtyrange{28}{30}{\mega\hertz}$ cannot cover the whole width of the $\qty{70}{\centi\meter}$ band.

<margin>
[picture:651:a_konverter_2:Up-converter for QO-100]
</margin>

---

A converter may therefore have to be switchable, as shown in Figure [ref:a_konverter], in order to cover larger frequency ranges. If, for example, a frequency range of $\qtyrange{436}{440}{\mega\hertz}$, i.e. a bandwidth of $\qty{4}{\mega\hertz}$, is to be converted to a frequency range of $\qtyrange{28}{30}{\mega\hertz}$ with $\qty{2}{\mega\hertz}$ (assuming that the oscillator frequency is below the wanted signal), two switchable frequency ranges are needed: the first from $\qtyrange{436}{438}{\mega\hertz}$ and the second from $\qtyrange{438}{440}{\mega\hertz}$. 

<margin>
[picture:85:a_konverter:Converter with switchable oscillator frequency]
</margin>

For the first sub-band, from $\qtyrange{436}{438}{\mega\hertz}$, the following oscillator frequency can be calculated: 

$f_\mathrm{OSZ} = \qty{436}{\mega\hertz}$ - $\qty{28}{\mega\hertz} = \qty{408}{\mega\hertz}$

$f_\mathrm{OSZ} = \qty{438}{\mega\hertz}$ - $\qty{30}{\mega\hertz} = \qty{408}{\mega\hertz}$

Not surprisingly, both band edges give an oscillator frequency of $\qty{408}{\mega\hertz}$.

For the second sub-band, from $\qtyrange{438}{440}{\mega\hertz}$, the oscillator frequency is:

$f_\mathrm{OSZ} = \qty{440}{\mega\hertz} - \qty{30}{\mega\hertz} = \qty{438}{\mega\hertz} - \qty{28}{\mega\hertz} = \qty{410}{\mega\hertz}$.

If this oscillator frequency is generated by frequency multiplication, this must also be taken into account, by dividing, when working back to the frequency needed from the crystal oscillator.

If the $\qty{408}{\mega\hertz}$ and $\qty{410}{\mega\hertz}$ calculated above are obtained by multiplying the crystal oscillator frequency by nine, the two crystal oscillator frequencies are $f_\mathrm{Quarz,1}=\frac{\qty{408}{\mega\hertz}}{9} = \qty{45.333}{\mega\hertz}$ and $f_\mathrm{Quarz,2}=\frac{\qty{410}{\mega\hertz}}{9} = \qty{45.556}{\mega\hertz}$ (each rounded).
