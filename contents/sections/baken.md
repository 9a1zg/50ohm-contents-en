A "beacon" is an automatically operating amateur radio transmitter (also in satellites) that automatically produces continuously repeated transmissions for observing field strength or for reception tests.

Beacons always transmit on a fixed assigned frequency and always from a fixed location. Like repeaters, they transmit their call sign at regular intervals for identification, usually in Morse telegraphy. Some beacons can also be time-synchronised and transmit further information.

[question:VD119]

Since whether a beacon can be received depends strongly on the changing propagation conditions of the band concerned, a beacon is a good indicator of whether a contact in a particular direction is feasible. If, for example, you can hear a beacon from South America well on HF, this is a sign that a contact there is now possible too. "Aurora beacons" on VHF help to find out whether DX contacts into the Scandinavian countries are currently possible using radio reflections from the aurora borealis. With known beacons on the VHF, UHF and SHF bands, you can check the alignment of your antenna by direction finding, and also check that your receiving system is working correctly.

The International Beacon Project (IBP) consists of a fairly large number of beacons spread over several countries on all continents. In a fixed time sequence, they transmit one after another for a few seconds each, all on the same frequency of an HF band. This lets you get an overview of the current propagation conditions on the band concerned very quickly.

Beacons are allocated special frequency ranges in the IARU band plans. Beacon frequencies should never be used for operating. Table [ref:n_baken_frequenzbereiche] gives the beacon frequencies of the HF range. All other designated beacon frequency ranges can be found in the band plans of the frequency band concerned.
