We have learnt that electrical oscillations consist of AC voltage, which alternately takes on positive and negative polarity. Usually, however, the voltage does not change abruptly from positive to negative and back. As you can see in Figure [ref:n_frequenz_sinusschwingung], the mains voltage, for example, swings again and again, smoothly like a pendulum, from plus through 0 to minus and back through 0 to plus.

<webmargin>
[picture:725:n_frequenz_sinusschwingung:The mains voltage over time. Incidentally, since the voltage does not have its peak value of $\qty{325}{\volt}$ all the time, its effective value is only $\qty{230}{\volt}$.]
</webmargin>
  
---

This basic form is called a sine wave [index:Oscillation:Sine wave]. It is easy to recognise by its wave-like shape. Besides sinusoidal AC voltages there are many other oscillations. The best known are the following:
* Square wave (Figure [ref:sinusschwingung_rechteck]) [index:Oscillation:Square wave]
* Triangular wave (Figure [ref:sinusschwingung_dreieck]) [index:Oscillation:Triangular wave]
* Sawtooth wave (Figure [ref:sinusschwingung_saegezahn]) [index:Oscillation:Sawtooth wave]
