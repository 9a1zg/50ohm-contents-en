The *ionosphere* lies in the upper part of the earth's atmosphere. There, radiation from the sun produces electrically charged particles. Short waves can be refracted in these areas and bent back to earth. This makes reception over hundreds or thousands of kilometres possible (see Figure [ref:swl_ionosphaere_ausbreitung]).

Propagation changes with the time of day, the season, the frequency and solar activity. A major long-term influence is the solar cycle of about eleven years (see [ref:swl_sonnenzyklus]). In phases of high solar activity there are more sunspots, and as a result higher frequencies in the HF range become usable over long distances more often.

[question:NH201]

---

The lower HF bands are often particularly interesting in the evening and at night. The higher HF bands tend to open during the day. These are rules of thumb, not guarantees: it is precisely the changes that make listening exciting.

Between the range of the ground wave and the point where the sky wave first comes down, there can be a *skip zone* (see [ref:swl_ionosphaere_ausbreitung]). You may then hear only one station of a QSO, although the two receive each other well.
