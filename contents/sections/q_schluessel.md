Q codes are something special in the world of radio. They were laid down very early on and are correspondingly deeply rooted in many radio services, not only in amateur radio. They were created above all so that terms that come up again and again could be conveyed quickly and easily.

The most important international set of rules for radio is the Radio Regulations (RR). The Q codes were laid down there, but amateur radio has adapted some of them to its own needs.

All Q codes are 3 characters long and always begin with the letter Q. They can be used as a question, as an answer or as a request.

It is often possible to add a question mark and so turn a statement into a question. For example, while "QRV" means that you are ready to receive, "QRV?" asks the other station whether it is ready to receive.

Some Q codes must be followed by a specific piece of information. For example, "QTH", which means roughly "My location is ...", must be followed by your own location. A correct use would therefore be, for example, "QTH Berlin".

[question:VA407]

In the following we discuss all the Q codes that appear in the examination, give memory aids and also explain the usual use in amateur radio where it differs from the original meaning. Table [ref:n_q_gruppen] also summarises this information compactly.

* QRV: I am ready (in German "ich bin *v*orbereitet", "I am prepared")
* QRM: Interference, or as a question: Are you being interfered with? Are you being disturbed by "*m*an-made" interference? This means interference caused by people or by equipment operated by people, e.g. a photovoltaic system or a transmission by a radio amateur on an adjacent frequency.
* QTH: My location ("*H*ome") is ..., e.g. "QTH Berlin" means roughly "My location is Berlin".

[question:BB204]

* QRT: Stop sending (*t*erminate)! In everyday amateur radio, many radio amateurs end their transmission with the remark "I am going QRT".
* QRZ?: This asks who has called you (a request to the other station: please give your call sign a second time, in German "ein *z*weites Mal")
* QSL: means acknowledgement of receipt, or as a question: a request for acknowledgement of receipt (everything *l*ogged?)
 
[question:BB203]

"QRZ?" strictly stands for the question "Who is calling me?". On the amateur bands, however, it is often heard in place of "CQ". This mostly happens in what is known as a "pile-up", when many stations are trying to reach a sought-after station. After the sought-after station has "worked through" a caller, it briefly sends "QRZ?" to invite further stations to call.

[question:BE115]

* QRO: stands for high power (in German "gr*o*ße Leistung"), or as a question: Shall I increase transmitter power? It is perhaps easier to remember as: "Shall I put a few more watts *o*n top?"
* QSO: In everyday use, the abbreviation is used for a contact. That is a loose but common interpretation. Strictly, "QSO?" means: "Can you communicate with ... direct?"
* QRX: I will call you again. As a question (QRX?): When will you call me again? Will you call me again at time *X*?

[question:BB202]

<margin>
Some radio amateurs try to bridge the greatest possible distances with particularly low transmit power – usually less than $\qty{5}{\watt}$. This is called *QRP operation*, after the Q code of the same name. Telegraphy using CW or digital modes is particularly well suited to QRP operation.
</margin>

* QRP: Even if you have only recently taken up amateur radio, you may already have heard the term "QRP". When we talk about QRP equipment or QRP contacts, we mean equipment or contacts with low transmit power. The original meaning is obvious: "QRP" is the request to reduce transmit power.

[question:BB205]

* QRN: Atmospheric interference. *N*atural interference is a good memory aid and a good way of telling it apart from QRM, man-made interference.
* QSB: means fading, or fluctuating signals. Think of "*b*ergauf und *b*ergab" (German for "uphill and downhill").
* QSY: Change of frequency, "Change Frequenc*y*"
