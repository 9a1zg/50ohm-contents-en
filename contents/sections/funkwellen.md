If an electrical oscillation is fed to an antenna, the antenna radiates a radio wave. Radio waves, just like light, are electromagnetic waves [index:Electromagnetic wave] and therefore travel at the speed of light [index:Speed of light]. In what is called free space, where there are no obstacles, this is approximately $\num{300000}$ kilometres per second.

[question:NB301]

---

Radio waves consist of crests and troughs. The higher the crests and the deeper the troughs, the stronger the radio signal. In this context we speak of the field strength [index:Field strength]. Here too, as with the electrical oscillation, the height of a crest or the depth of a trough is called the amplitude.

<attention>
Electromagnetic waves are often shown as a *snapshot*. At first sight, snapshots look just like the oscillograms we have already discussed. But they show something different! While an oscillogram shows how the voltage changes over time, a snapshot shows only a single point in time, a moment. You can read off how the field strength changes with distance. Look carefully at the label of the horizontal x-axis. On an oscillogram it says $t$ for time (Latin tempus); on a snapshot, by contrast, it says "position".
</attention>

<attention>
Do not let the way a *snapshot* is drawn mislead you into thinking that the electromagnetic wave dances up and down in space. The vertical y-axis does not show a spatial displacement up or down but the field strength. The further the curve is from the zero line, the greater the field strength.
</attention>
