
Installing amateur radio equipment in a motor vehicle makes driving that much more fun. Via repeaters you are well connected with other amateur stations and often get valuable information about the traffic situation on your route. For operating while driving, a hands-free kit is needed – as with a smartphone.

<margin>
[photo:75:n_mobilfunkgeraet:Control unit of a VHF/UHF radio installed in the centre console of a car]
</margin>

Installing equipment and antennas in a car must be carefully planned and carried out. When connecting the supply voltage to the radio, installing the antenna and routing the cables in the vehicle, the vehicle manufacturer's instructions must be observed, as otherwise the road approval of the vehicle is put at risk.

[question:NK308]

---

For a mobile antenna to radiate its signals well, it should be mounted as close as possible to the middle of the metal roof of the vehicle.  The vehicle roof then forms the counterpoise, similar to the earth in a Marconi antenna. In the simplest case a *magnetic-mount antenna* is used. Vehicle manufacturers often also specify where antennas may be installed.

<margin>
[photo:64:n_magnetfussantenne:Magnetic-mount antenna on a vehicle roof]
</margin>

[question:NK310]

If the antenna cables of a mobile installation are routed parallel and very close to other cables in the vehicle, the transmitted signal can interfere with the vehicle electronics. Antenna cables should therefore be as short as possible and routed as far as possible from other vehicle wiring. Crossing other cables, on the other hand, is often not critical. 

[question:NK309]

---

The vehicle supply voltage in a motor vehicle seems harmless at first, as touching voltages of $\qty{12}{\volt}$ or $\qty{24}{\volt}$ cannot give you an electric shock. But it must also be borne in mind that a vehicle battery can deliver very high currents. In the event of a short circuit, an arc, a cable fire or even a vehicle fire can quickly result. We will look more closely at the *difference between voltage and current* in the chapter on power supplies. 

<margin>
[photo:76:n_Kabelsicherung:Power cable with fuseholder]
</margin>

%[class:N]
<indepth>
An *arc* is an electrical discharge, that is, a flow of current, through air that is normally non-conducting, accompanied by high temperatures and a very bright glow. It can result in fires and damage to the eyes.
</indepth>
%[/class]

A fuse of the correct rating must always be fitted in the supply lead of the vehicle radio! Figure [ref:n_Kabelsicherung] shows a typical fuseholder with a fuse inserted. In high-quality equipment, fuses are fitted in both leads, one for the positive and one for the negative terminal. This gives better protection than a single fuse in the lead to the positive terminal.
