We have also already learnt that as soon as you connect an electrical load such as a lamp to the two terminals of a voltage source, the separated electric charges start to move. We call this a *closed circuit*, in which an electric current flows. Depending on how large the voltage is and what kind of load is connected, more or less current flows. We speak of the *electric current* and state it in the unit ampere, with the abbreviation $\unit{\ampere}$.

<person>
The unit ampere is named after the French physicist and mathematician *André-Marie Ampère*, who gave a scientific explanation of electric voltage and electric current in the 1820s.
</person>

Let us look at some examples again: a current of $\qty{5}{\milli\ampere}$, for example, flows through a light-emitting diode (LED). For a transceiver receiving, it is already $\qty{900}{\milli\ampere}$, for example, i.e. almost one ampere. Considerably more current can flow through a transceiver that is transmitting, for example $\qty{21}{\ampere}$.

To answer the following question, we need to be clear that $\qty{1}{\milli\ampere}$ is the same as $\qty{0.001}{\ampere}$. When working it out, however, you have to be careful not to get mixed up with the zeros after the decimal point: if $\qty{1}{\milli\ampere}$ is the same as $\qty{0.001}{\ampere}$, then $\qty{10}{\milli\ampere}$ is the same as $\qty{0.010}{\ampere}$. It follows that $\qty{40}{\milli\ampere}$ is $\qty{0.040}{\ampere}$. And finally, $\qty{42}{\milli\ampere}$ therefore corresponds to $\qty{0.042}{\ampere}$.
