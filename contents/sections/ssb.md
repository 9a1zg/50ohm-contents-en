We have learnt that in amplitude modulation there are two sidebands in addition to the carrier. They are called the lower and the upper sideband. As can be seen in Figure [ref:n_seitenband_2], the "lower sideband" (LSB) lies below the carrier in frequency, and the "upper sideband" (USB) above it. 

<margin>
[picture:482:n_seitenband_2:Amplitude modulation, carrier with lower (a) and upper (b) sideband]
</margin>

<attention>
In German, the common abbreviation *USB* is easily taken for the lower sideband (*unteres Seitenband*); it stands, as the English name says, for the *upper sideband*!
</attention>

It is remarkable that all the information of the modulating signal is contained entirely in the sidebands, mirrored identically in the lower and the upper sideband. The carrier itself contains no information at all! So in fact it is enough to transmit only one sideband and to do without the carrier and the other sideband. This is exactly what is done in single-sideband modulation, *SSB* for short. Technically, this can be achieved, for example, by suppressing the carrier during modulation and filtering out the wanted sideband.

The advantage of SSB is that no transmit power is "wasted" on the carrier, which is actually superfluous, and on the second sideband. Instead, all the transmit power can be used to transmit the information.

---

Depending on which sideband is transmitted, the method of transmission is called LSB or USB. In LSB only the lower sideband is transmitted, and in USB only the upper sideband. The sideband is selected on the transceiver.

<webmargin>
[picture:743:n_seitenband_3:Sidebands in AM, LSB and USB compared]
</webmargin>

In SSB there is practically no emission during pauses in speech, since the carrier is suppressed. The bandwidth occupied by SSB is equal to the bandwidth of the signal modulated onto the carrier. In the SSB examples in Figure [ref:n_seitenband_3], that is $\qty{2700}{\hertz}$, and so a little less than half the bandwidth of AM.
