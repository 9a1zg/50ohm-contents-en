The AC voltage in our mains supply *oscillates* back and forth 50 times a second. The number of oscillations [index:Oscillation] per second is called the frequency [index:Frequency]. Frequency is given in the unit hertz [index:Unit:Hertz], abbreviated $\unit{\hertz}$. One $\unit{\hertz}$ corresponds to exactly one oscillation per second. So the mains supply has a frequency of $\qty{50}{\hertz}$.

<unit>
Frequency is measured in the *unit hertz*. One hertz equals one oscillation per second. The unit was named after the German physicist Heinrich Rudolf Hertz, who in 1886 was the first person to generate and detect electromagnetic waves.
  
$$\qty{1}{\hertz} = \frac{1}{\unit{\second}}$$
  
</unit>

In radio we also use electrical oscillations, but at much higher frequencies than the mains, e.g. $\qty{144000000}{\hertz}$. With so many digits it quickly becomes hard to read. So we use the term megahertz ($\unit{\mega\hertz}$) for one million hertz and say "144 megahertz". We have used the *unit prefix* "$\unit{\mega\,}$" and placed it in front of the actual unit "$\unit{\hertz}$". This expresses that the value given is multiplied by one million. Further examples of frequencies with unit prefixes are collected in Table [ref:n_frequenz_einheitenvorzeichen] [index:Unit prefix].

In Class N, the entry-level amateur radio class, you may, for example, use the frequencies from $\num{28}$ to $\qty{29.7}{\mega\hertz}$, from $\num{144}$ to $\qty{146}{\mega\hertz}$ and from $\qty{430}{\mega\hertz}$ to $\qty{440}{\mega\hertz}$. Class E and Class A add further frequency ranges.

In radios, electrical oscillations are generated by what is called an oscillator. When you press the transmit button on the radio, the oscillations are passed to the antenna and radiated there as radio waves.

<wordorigin>
The term *oscillator* [index:Oscillator] comes from the Latin word oscillare, which means roughly "to swing".
</wordorigin>
