To explain frequency and radio waves, we have to go back a little and take a brief look at electrical voltage. All the materials we know consist of tiny particles - wood, water, metals and everything else, even the air. Some of these particles have the property of attracting or repelling each other. They are called "electrically charged" and are divided into the groups "positive" (plus) and "negative" (minus)  (Figure [ref:n_frequenz_elektrische_ladungen]) [index:Charge].

<margin>
[picture:713:n_frequenz_elektrische_ladungen:Positively and negatively charged particles evenly distributed in an object.]
</margin>

---

Particles with the same charge repel each other: plus repels plus and minus repels minus. Opposite charges, on the other hand, attract each other: plus and minus attract each other (Figure [ref:n_ladungen]).  Because plus and minus attract each other, most objects are electrically balanced, i.e. they contain equal numbers of positive and negative charges. The sum is zero.

<margin>
[picture:710:n_ladungen:Attraction and repulsion of charges]
</margin>

---

Charges can, however, be deliberately separated, e.g. in a battery, a solar cell or a wind turbine. The charges then try to come back together. There is an electrical *voltage* [index:Voltage]. Devices that separate charges and thus provide an electrical voltage are called *voltage sources* [index:Voltage:Voltage source]. The positively charged terminal is called the positive terminal and the other the negative terminal. Voltage sources can have voltages of different sizes. Electrical voltage is measured in the unit volt [index:Unit:Volt], abbreviated $\unit{V}$.

As soon as an electrical load [index:Load] such as a lamp or a computer is connected to the two terminals and switched on, the charges can move. An electric current [index:Current] flows (Figure [ref:n_frequenz_strom_fliesst]).

<margin>
[picture:714:n_frequenz_strom_fliesst:The terminals of a battery: at the negative terminal there is an excess of negative charges and at the positive terminal an excess of positive charges; the terminals of the battery are connected, so the current can flow through the load.]
</margin>

There are also voltage sources whose terminals constantly oscillate between positive and negative voltage, for example the power stations of the public mains supply. What comes out of a household socket is therefore AC voltage [index:Voltage:AC voltage] - as opposed to DC voltage [index:Voltage:DC voltage], with its positive and negative terminals.
