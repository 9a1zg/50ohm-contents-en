We have already met electric voltage. Let us sum up once more: if positive charges are deliberately separated from negative ones, e.g. in a battery, these charges try to come back together. An electric voltage is then present, e.g. between the terminals of the battery. Electric voltage is measured in the unit volt, abbreviated $\unit{V}$.

<person>
The unit volt is named after the Italian physicist *Alessandro Volta*.
</person>

In radio engineering, both very large and very small voltages occur. As with frequency, unit prefixes help us to state large values. $\qty{1}{\kilo\hertz}$ stands for $\qty{1000}{\hertz}$. It is the same with voltages: $\qty{1}{\kilo\volt}$ means $\qty{1000}{\volt}$. The most important unit prefixes are shown in Table [ref:n_spannung_einheitenvorzeichen], using the unit volt.

But there are also unit prefixes for very small values. We know this, for example, from the millilitre ($\unit{\milli\liter}$): 1 litre is $\qty{1000}{\milli\liter}$. Let us look at some examples of large and small voltages. A particularly small voltage is present at an antenna during reception. The radio waves picked up are so weak that the electrical oscillations have a voltage of only about $\qty{10}{\micro\volt}$. When we speak into a microphone, somewhat higher voltages of approx. $\qty{200}{\milli\volt}$ are produced.

The voltage usually present at a battery is a little higher, for example $\qty{1.5}{\volt}$ or $\qty{9}{\volt}$. The voltage at household mains sockets is higher still: it is as much as $\qty{230}{\volt}$. During thunderstorms or rain, an antenna can become electrically charged. A voltage of, for example, $\qty{1.5}{\kilo\volt}$ can then be present on it. Particularly high voltages are found on the high-voltage lines of the electricity grid. Here you can encounter up to $\qty{380}{\kilo\volt}$.

For the next question we must remember that $\qty{1}{\volt}$ is the same as $\qty{1000}{\milli\volt}$. Then the question should be easy to answer.
