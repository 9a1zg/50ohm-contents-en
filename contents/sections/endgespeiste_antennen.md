Instead of at the centre, the antenna cable can also be connected at one end of the dipole [ref:n_antennenformen_schaltbild_endfed]. This antenna is then called an *end-fed antenna*, or an *end-fed* for short.

<margin>
[picture:615:n_antennenformen_schaltbild_endfed:Circuit diagram of an end-fed antenna]
</margin>

A common design is the end-fed half-wave dipole. The length of the wire is then $\lambda / 2$, the same as the total length of the normal half-wave dipole. For the antenna to work, a special matching network must be used. 

<indepth>
% TODO: Editionsspezifisch machen
The *matching network* is needed because, unlike the antennas presented so far, this antenna does not have the right feed-point impedance. 
What that is will be covered later in this chapter. How a matching network works is explained in the Class E course.
</indepth>

<indepth>
The end-fed is a popular type of antenna. It has the advantage that the feeder is connected not at the centre but at one of the ends.
</indepth>

<indepth>
Where, actually, is the *counterpoise of the end-fed antenna*? After all, we have learnt that an antenna always consists of two parts. With the end-fed antenna, part of the feeder is often used as the counterpoise. However, this can lead to interference to electrical equipment and in your own radio installation, too. Such interference can be avoided, for example, by a $\lambda / 4$ long radial as the counterpoise, a good connection to earth and the use of a common-mode choke.
</indepth>

[question:NG107]

In the HF range, particularly long end-fed antennas are often used. An end-fed antenna whose wire is longer than $\lambda$ is called a *long-wire antenna*.  Long-wire antennas are not usually used in the VHF/UHF range.

<indepth>
The term *long-wire antenna* is sometimes also wrongly used for the end-fed half-wave dipole, although this is shorter than $\lambda$.
</indepth>
