When calculating safety distances, off-axis attenuation plays an important part with directional antennas. Most power is radiated in the centre of the radiation lobe. In other directions it is lower. If the antenna is high enough, it radiates for the most part over the area that is <u>not</u> controllable, that is, the area in which the limits must be complied with without fail. 

<margin>
[picture:950:a_richtantenne_personenschutz:At an angle of $\qty{40}{\degree}$ below the axis of the main lobe, the radiated power is $\qty{6}{\decibel}$ lower than at an angle of $\qty{0}{\degree}$.]
</margin>

Figure [ref:a_richtantenne_personenschutz] shows, at the critical angle of $\qty{40}{\degree}$ below the antenna, an area that is not controllable, in which people may be present. The radiated power there is $\qty{6}{\dB}$ lower than in the centre of the radiation pattern. The direct consequence is that the safety distance there can be correspondingly smaller.

$\qty{6}{\dB}$ corresponds to a factor of $\num{0.25}$ or $\dfrac{1}{4}$ (formula sheet).

$ E = \dfrac{\sqrt{\qty{30}{\ohm}\cdot P_\textrm{EIRP}}}{d}$
Rearranging the formula for $d$ (safety distance).
$ d = \dfrac{\sqrt{\qty{30}{\ohm}\cdot P_\textrm{EIRP}}}{E}$

The radiated power $P_\textrm{EIRP}$ is not known. We do know, however, that in this calculation we only have to use a quarter of the maximum radiated power.

$\begin{split} d &= \dfrac{\sqrt{\qty{30}{\ohm}\cdot P_\textrm{EIRP}\cdot \dfrac{1}{4}}}{E}\\ d &= \dfrac{\sqrt{\qty{30}{\ohm}\cdot P_\textrm{EIRP}}}{E}\cdot \sqrt{\dfrac{1}{4}}\\ d &= \dfrac{\sqrt{\qty{30}{\ohm}\cdot P_\textrm{EIRP}}}{E}\cdot \mathbf{\dfrac{1}{2}}\end{split}$

If the radiated power is reduced to $\dfrac{1}{4}$, the safety distance of $\qty{20}{\meter}$ is halved. In this particular example it is reduced to $\qty{10}{\meter}$.
