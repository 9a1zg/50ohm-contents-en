In every household and every amateur station there are a large number of electrically conductive objects. These can be earth leads, water pipes, heating pipes, coaxial cables, metal cases, etc. Faults in electrical installations, mistakes made during work on an electrical installation, electrostatic charges, lightning strikes and so on can unexpectedly put potentials on these conductors. Between the different conductors, this leads to potential differences. On touching them, you may be exposed to dangerous voltages. 
This can be remedied by connecting together all the conductive parts in a room or area. This creates equipotential bonding and **protects persons** from electric shock. The connections must be **low-resistance** to reliably prevent potential differences.

[question:AK202]

We earth the equipment of our station in order to carry external potentials safely away. Even so, unpleasant surprises are possible. RF keeps finding its own paths whenever it has the chance. 

If the transmitter case is connected by a separate lead to the equipotential bonding busbar in the basement, the length of the lead may, on some amateur bands, be λ/4 of the transmitted wavelength or an odd multiple of it. The earthing conductor is then resonant at the transmit frequency. 

Since the end of the lead is earthed, no voltage antinode can form there. It therefore builds up at the beginning, that is, where the earthing conductor is connected to the transmitter. As a result, a high voltage, depending on the transmit power, is present on the case. Touching it gives you an electric shock or burns. 

Radio amateurs say the case is "hot". You flinch back, just as you flinch back when you touch a hot hotplate.

<tip>
What do you do if a separate earthing conductor is resonant at the transmit frequency? You can change the length of the lead so that it is not resonant on any of the amateur bands. If necessary, an existing earthing conductor must be lengthened.
</tip>
