As we have already learnt in Classes N and E, in frequency modulation the information of the modulating signal is contained not in the amplitude but only in the change in frequency of the carrier signal. So only the zero crossings of the carrier signal have to be evaluated in the receiver. 

Amplitude fluctuations are removed by a limiting amplifier in the process. Frequency modulation is therefore, by its very nature, insensitive to impulse interference to the amplitude, caused for example by ignition sparks, electric motors or the like. FM is therefore well suited to operation in motor vehicles.

[question:AE302]

In Class A we will now look at how frequency modulation can be generated in a transmitter and how the bandwidth of an FM signal can be calculated.

---

Frequency modulation can be generated by changing the capacitance of the frequency-determining capacitor in an oscillator (see Figure [ref:fm_modulation_schaltung]). For example, frequency modulation can be generated with a varactor diode in series with a tuned circuit or crystal. The amplitude of the audio frequency (AF), produced e.g. by a microphone and applied to the varactor diode, directly determines the change in frequency of the oscillator.

The modulating frequency determines how often the frequency of the oscillator changes.

[question:AE301]

In Class E we met the *frequency deviation*. It gives the amount by which the modulating signal moves the instantaneous frequency of the FM signal away from the carrier frequency. The larger the amplitude of the modulating signal, the greater this frequency excursion.

In demodulation in the FM receiver, this frequency excursion is converted back into a corresponding amplitude of the demodulated signal. Other things being equal, a greater frequency deviation therefore leads to a larger amplitude of the demodulated AF signal.

A greater frequency deviation increases the bandwidth the FM signal needs. If the intended values are exceeded, the transmitted signal can then extend into neighbouring channels and so cause adjacent-channel interference.

---

Strictly speaking, the occupied bandwidth of an FM emission is determined not only by the deviation but also by the maximum modulating frequency (see Figure [ref:fm_modulation]). As a first approximation, for small deviation and a low modulating frequency, Carson's rule can be applied. It gives the bandwidth that contains $\qty{99}{\percent}$ of the transmit power.

$B\approx2 \cdot \left(\Delta f_{\textrm{T}} + f_{\textrm{mod max}} \right)$

</margin>

With Carson's rule, the occupied bandwidth of an FM emission can be calculated when the deviation and the modulating frequency are known. By rearranging the formula, each of the other quantities can be calculated too.
