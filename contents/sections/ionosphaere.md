The *ionosphere* [index:Ionosphere] lies in the upper part of the earth's atmosphere. It has a great influence on radio wave propagation in the HF range.

Radiation from the sun creates electrically charged particles in the ionosphere. Electromagnetic waves are bent (refracted) at these electrically charged particles and so directed back to the earth (Figure [ref:n_ionosphaere]). In this way, long ranges can be achieved in the HF range and worldwide contacts become possible. Radio waves refracted at the ionosphere are called the sky wave [index:Sky wave] – in contrast to the ground wave [index:Ground wave], which follows the curvature of the earth but usually does not reach as far as the sky wave.

Propagation conditions are subject to daily and seasonal change. The eleven-year solar cycle [index:Solar cycle] has a major influence on HF propagation. At the peak of the cycle, a particularly large number of what are called sunspots [index:Solar cycle:Sunspots] appear (Figure [ref:n_ionosphaere_sonnenflecken]). The sun then emits a particularly large amount of electromagnetic radiation and matter and has a strong influence on the ionosphere, and so on radio conditions.

When operating on the HF bands, a *skip zone* [index:Skip zone] may form. This means distances that are too far for the ground wave and too near for the sky wave (Figure [ref:n_ionosphaere_tote_zone]). As a result, you can often hear only one of the stations taking part in a contact in progress, because the other is in the skip zone. This can at first give the false impression that a frequency is free.
