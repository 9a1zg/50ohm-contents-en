Many radios have what is known as a DATA port, which is often also marked *DATA* or *$\qty{9600}{\baud}$*, as shown in Figure [ref:e_9600_port]. This port was originally developed for packet radio applications, which today have largely been replaced by HAMNET. Nevertheless, the port can also be used for other methods, for example the digital voice transmission method M17. In this case a suitable modem – often also called a TNC – is connected, as shown in Figure [ref:m17_tnc].

The $\qty{9600}{\baud}$ port provides a direct connection to the transceiver's modulator and demodulator, so that the signals are processed with high precision and low distortion. For higher data rates, such as those used for digital signals in $\qty{9600}{\baud}$ packet radio (AX.25 protocol), for example, it is necessary to bypass the entire audio path with its limited frequency response and filtering (e.g. microphone filter and preamplifier). The audio path of a transceiver is normally tailored to speech signals and has a restricted bandwidth, often between $\qty{300}{\hertz}$ and $\qty{3000}{\hertz}$. This bandwidth is not enough to transmit $\qty{9600}{\baud}$ reliably, because such a high rate needs a wider signal bandwidth. Via the data port, the signals are transferred without the filters, the DSP processing and the de-emphasis that are present in the audio path. This reduces distortion and latency, which is critical for digital transmissions in order to keep the error rate to a minimum. 

In short: the $\qty{9600}{\baud}$ port is intended specifically for processing digital data directly and without the limitations of the audio path, which is necessary for reliable and efficient high-speed data transmission.

So why $\qty{9600}{\baud}$?
  
$\qty{9600}{\baud}$ ($\qty{9.6}{\kilo\bit\per\second}$ if a modulation with one bit per symbol is used) is a common speed for digital communication in amateur radio, particularly in packet radio. This data rate is a compromise between the speed that can be achieved and what is technically feasible in the VHF/UHF frequency range, where most FM transceivers operate.
