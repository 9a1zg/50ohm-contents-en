In the chapter on resistor colour codes we already briefly met the term tolerance. The tolerance of a resistor describes by how much its actual value may deviate from the nominal, i.e. printed, value. It is usually given as a percentage and defines the range within which the real resistance value can lie.

For example, a tolerance of $\qty{\pm 5}{\percent}$ means that the actual resistance value can lie between $\qty{95}{\percent}$ and $\qty{105}{\percent}$ of the stated value.

Suppose you have a resistor with a nominal value of $\qty{1000}{\ohm}$ ($\qty{1}{\kilo\ohm}$) and a tolerance of $\qty{\pm 5}{\percent}$. Since $\qty{5}{\percent}$ of $\qty{1000}{\ohm}$ is exactly $\qty{50}{\ohm}$, the actual resistance value can deviate by $\qty{\pm 50}{\ohm}$. The actual resistance value of this $\qty{1}{\kilo\ohm}$ resistor with $\qty{5}{\percent}$ tolerance could therefore lie anywhere between $\qty{950}{\ohm}$ and $\qty{1050}{\ohm}$.

The most common tolerances for resistors are $\qty{\pm 1}{\percent}$, $\qty{\pm 2}{\percent}$, $\qty{\pm 5}{\percent}$ and $\qty{\pm 10}{\percent}$. Resistors with a smaller tolerance (e.g. $\qty{\pm 1}{\percent}$) are more accurate, but often more expensive too. Tolerances are particularly important where circuits require high precision, as in measuring instruments or sensitive sensors. In such cases, resistors with a small tolerance ($\qty{\pm 1}{\percent}$ or less) are often used.
