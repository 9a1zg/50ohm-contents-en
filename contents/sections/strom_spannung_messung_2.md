In Class N we have already met ammeters and voltmeters and their circuit symbols. A current or voltage measurement should affect the circuit as little as possible, since the circuit normally operates without the meters. For this purpose, ammeters and voltmeters have *internal resistances* of different values. The internal resistance indicates how much the flow of current through the meter is limited, and in principle corresponds to an ordinary resistor. The internal resistance is also given in ohms.

---

An ammeter is connected in series with the component being measured, e.g. a resistor. The same current therefore flows through the component as through the meter. So that the flow of current is affected as little as possible by the meter, the ammeter has a *low* internal resistance.

Voltmeters, in contrast, have a *high* internal resistance. The meter is connected into the circuit in parallel. It thus connects two different points in the circuit. The voltage displayed is then a measure of the degree of charge separation between these two points. A current through this parallel branch would, however, break down the charge separation again, and this is prevented by the high internal resistance.

<indepth>
Ideally, an ammeter would have a resistance of $\qty{0}{\ohm}$ and a voltmeter a resistance of $\qty{\infty}{\ohm}$. In practice, however, this cannot be achieved. We will look at exactly this in a little more detail in Class A.
</indepth>
