The frequency on a radio is set with a tuning knob or with buttons. Only stations transmitting on the frequency you have set can be heard from the receiver. Stations transmitting on higher or lower frequencies cannot be heard. If you want to find out whether a station is transmitting anywhere, you have to "tune across the band" slowly and listen carefully. 

Besides the frequency display, modern radios also offer an "amplitude spectrum" [index:Amplitude spectrum] (spectrum display) and a "waterfall display" [index:Waterfall display]. With these you can see at a glance whether other stations are transmitting above or below the set frequency, and how strong they are. Both displays show a frequency range on the horizontal axis. In the figures this is a segment of the $\qty{2}{\meter}$ band. In the middle of this axis is the set frequency, on which a strong received signal can be seen at the moment.

In the *amplitude spectrum* (Figure [ref:n_wasserfall_amplitudenspektrum]), the stronger a signal is, the higher the amplitude shown above the axis. If stations are active, you can see them immediately in the amplitude spectrum.

The *waterfall display* (Figure [ref:n_wasserfall_wasserfall]), on the other hand, shows on the vertical axis how the signal amplitudes vary over time, as colour and/or brightness. Since this display – like a waterfall – slowly runs from top to bottom, you can easily see when a transmission starts and when it ends again. The more intense the colour and the brighter the waterfall, the stronger the signal.

The amplitude spectrum must not be confused with the oscillogram, which we have already met. The amplitude spectrum shows the amplitudes for different frequencies along the horizontal axis, i.e. from left to right. So you can see what share the different frequencies have in the overall signal. The oscillogram, on the other hand, shows the amplitudes at different points in time. From left to right you see how the signal varies over time.
