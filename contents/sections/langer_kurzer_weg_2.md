In Class E we have already met the concept of the short and long path. In Class A we would like to go into this topic a little more deeply. A straight-line connection between two places on a sphere always runs along the great circle. The directions of the short and the long path to the other place therefore differ by exactly $\qty{180}{\degree}$. Figure [ref:a_langer_kurzer_weg] shows the great circle on the globe – the short path in blue and the long path in red, which runs all the way round the globe.

For directional antennas (beams) there are so-called great-circle maps (beam heading maps), which show the paths of all possible great circles for a particular starting point. They use an azimuthal equidistant projection, that is, a projection of the globe onto a disc in which both the distance and the direction of every point from the centre of the map are shown exactly. Radio amateurs use this kind of map to turn their directional antenna in the right direction. The angle given corresponds to the angle that typical rotator controllers also display. Figure [ref:a_beam_karte] shows such a great-circle map with Berlin as the location of the amateur station. That the map depends on the location of the station can be seen in Figure [ref:a_beam_karte_sydney], which shows a great-circle map for Sydney in Australia.

As Figure [ref:a_beam_karte] shows, the distance from Germany to Australia via the short path across East Asia is about $\qty{16000}{\kilo\meter}$, at an azimuth of about $\qty{75}{\degree}$. If you choose the long path instead, you point the antenna at $\qty{255}{\degree}$ and cover almost $\qty{24000}{\kilo\meter}$ – the signal path then runs across South America and the South Pacific.

Unfortunately, no great-circle map is available as an aid in the exam. We therefore recommend memorising the approximate angles of the continents – this will also be helpful later when operating on HF.
