Let us now come back to semiconductors. As we have already learnt, these are materials that conduct only under certain conditions. Semiconductors such as silicon or germanium have properties both of conductors and of non-conductors. How exactly they work is covered in the training material for Classes E and A.

The simplest component made of semiconductors is the *diode*, shown in Figure [ref:n_halbleiter_dioden]. The diode has the interesting property that current can flow through it *in one direction only*.

The terminals of a diode are called the *anode* and the *cathode*. If a wire in a circuit is replaced by a diode, current can flow in the direction from the anode to the cathode, but not the other way round. A diode behaves like an automatic switch: if you connect the positive terminal to the anode and the negative terminal to the cathode, the switch is closed and the diode conducts. But if you connect it the other way round, with the positive terminal to the cathode and the negative terminal to the anode, the diode behaves like an open switch and does not conduct.

Figure [ref:n_halbleiter_diode_merkhilfe] makes all this easy to remember: the diode symbol points like an arrow in the direction of conventional current flow. The terminal names anode and cathode are easy to remember if you add the letters A and K, the usual symbols for anode and cathode, to the diode symbol.

A special kind of diode is the light-emitting diode, or LED for short. This is a diode that lights up as soon as current flows through it. The circuit symbol of an LED is that of the diode with two additional arrows indicating the light (Figure [ref:n_halbleiter_led]). Apart from the fact that the LED lights up, it behaves exactly like any other diode: it lets current flow through it in one direction only.
