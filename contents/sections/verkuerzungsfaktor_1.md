In free space (a vacuum), and approximately also in air, wavelength ($\lambda$) and frequency ($f$) can, as we have learnt, be converted into each other using the speed of light in a vacuum ($c$):

$\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f}$

On lines, including antenna wires, on which waves form, the speed of light in a vacuum cannot be used. Instead, a correction factor is needed: the *velocity factor* $k_\mathrm{v}$ (for antennas usually called the *shortening factor*; German has one word for both, *Verkürzungsfaktor*), which is often of the order of $\qty{95}{\percent}$, i.e. $\num{0.95}$. It states how much more slowly propagation takes place *along a particular line* compared with free space, and it is multiplied by the speed of light in a vacuum:

$\lambda_\mathrm{Leitung} = k_\mathrm{v} \cdot \dfrac{c}{f}$

The velocity factor is therefore the ratio of the velocity of propagation along a line to the velocity of propagation in a vacuum.

---

The correction factor depends, among other things, on the wire diameter, the wire insulation (dielectric) and the effects of the surroundings.

<indepth>
As we will see later, only the dielectric used matters for the velocity factor of a *coaxial cable*, and the rest of the cable's geometry (e.g. its diameter) can be neglected.
</indepth>

Since the correction factor is often approx. $\num{0.95}$ (i.e. $\qty{95}{\percent}$), antennas often have to be shortened by about $\qty{5}{\percent}$ to make them resonant.
