In Class N we already met the Yagi-Uda directional antenna. This antenna works as follows: a signal is fed into a dipole, which is sometimes built as a folded dipole. The wave spreading out from this element, called the *radiator*, reaches both a somewhat longer *reflector* and a somewhat shorter *director* (see Figure [ref:e_yagi]). Many Yagi-Uda antennas also have several directors. The reflector and directors then begin to oscillate themselves, even though they have no direct electrical connection to the radiator.

The directional effect arises because the radiator and the other elements have a phase shift in time and in space, i.e. the crests and troughs of the waves do not occur at the same time or at the same place. The superposition of the (phase-shifted) radiation of all the elements produces a directional effect, because in certain directions the waves cancel each other out (destructive interference, where a crest meets a trough) and in other directions the waves reinforce each other (constructive interference, where a crest meets a crest and a trough meets a trough).

In the radiation pattern, this directional effect can be seen as a large *main lobe*, as shown in Figure [ref:e_yagi_richtung]. The main lobe points in the direction of maximum radiation, in which the signal, or the sensitivity when receiving, is mainly concentrated.

However, there can also be side lobes, and in particular a back lobe. That is, a Yagi-Uda antenna also radiates backwards to some extent, although this is actually unwanted. The back lobe and any side lobes are smaller, however.
