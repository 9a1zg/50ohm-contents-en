## Up-and-outer antenna
<left>
[picture:659:n_up_and_outer:Up-and-outer antenna]
</left>
<right>
* One leg of the dipole is placed vertically (*radiator*), the other horizontally (*radial*/*counterpoise*)
* Together $\lambda / 2$ long, i.e. $\lambda / 4$ each
* One of the *vertical antennas*
</right>
<note>
It is not only the radiator that radiates, but the whole structure of radiator and counterpoise. High field strengths (near field) can also occur at the radials - so take them into account when considering the safety distance!
</note>

---

Multiplying the radial gives a *ground-plane antenna*

<note>
On VHF, ground-plane antennas are popular for local contacts and repeater operation.
</note>

## Marconi antenna

<left>
[picture:669:n_marconi_antenne:Marconi antenna]

[picture:544:n_schaltzeichen_erde:Circuit symbol for earth]
</left>
<right>
* Radial is replaced by the ground
* Radiator still $\lambda / 4$ long
* Also called a "$\lambda / 4$ vertical antenna fed against earth"
</right>
<note>
In practice, e.g. with an earth spike (metal rod in the ground) or many buried wires like the radials of a ground plane
</note>

## Omnidirectional antennas
* Ground-plane and Marconi antennas radiate equally in all directions
* Unlike directional antennas, suitable for reaching stations in different directions without having to be re-aimed
* Should be positioned as high as possible and clear all round, e.g. on a mast or the roof of the house
