A worldwide community

Welcome to the fascinating world of amateur radio! As many as 2.8 million people are already part of the community of radio amateurs. We are dedicated to technical and scientific goals, but also to social goals and goals that promote education and development, and we serve international understanding and humanitarian tasks.

What is amateur radio?

As a participant in the amateur service, you have the right to develop and operate your own transmitting and receiving equipment. Amateur radio offers the opportunity to experiment with a wide range of transmission methods – from classic short wave and satellite communication through to the Internet of Things, including new methods using artificial intelligence. The amateur service offers not only the opportunity to broaden your own knowledge through experiments and continuous learning, but also allows you to contribute to global understanding. When needed, the amateur service can also support relief efforts in emergencies and disasters. It is important to stress that the amateur service stands on an equal footing with other radio services such as mobile communications, broadcasting and aeronautical radio.

How do you become a radio amateur?

To take part in the amateur service, you need an amateur radio certificate, which is obtained by passing an examination at the Federal Network Agency (Bundesnetzagentur, BNetzA). This platform is for preparing for the examinations for the various amateur radio classes. You can start here straight away.

Which amateur radio classes are there in Germany? 

In Germany, amateur radio is divided into three classes: Class N for newcomers, the extended Class E and Class A, with the highest power on all allocated frequencies. Three different classes ... so what is the difference in practice?

In this context, people very often also ask: "How far does it actually reach?" Or: "How far can I get with which class?" Unfortunately, there is no more a universally valid answer to that than to the question "How much does a computer cost?" (€20 for a Raspberry Pi Zero, many millions for a supercomputer). The question "How hard is the examination?" is just as hard to answer: a twelve-year-old pupil will naturally find it much more difficult than a professor of electrical engineering. Nevertheless, we will try to give a rough – and also greatly simplified – impression of which kinds of contact you can make with which class, and which ranges are realistic.

Class N

With Class N of the amateur service, newly introduced in mid-2024, you may operate on the 10 m band, the 2 m band and the 70 cm band. Because their frequencies are very similar, these bands are very much like the licence-free personal radio services (CB radio, Freenet and PMR) in their propagation conditions.

With Class N, however, you may work with up to 20 times the power, in particular compared with PMR and Freenet (10 W EIRP instead of 0.5 W EIRP or 1 W ERP), which benefits the achievable range considerably. Furthermore, amateur radio also allows other transmission methods such as SSB, Morse telegraphy or digital methods (e.g. FT-8, JS-8 or LoRA), which increase the range considerably compared with licence-free radio applications, even at the same transmit power. Communication with orbiting satellites that transmit on the 2 m and 70 cm bands is also possible.

In particular, amateur radio has numerous repeaters on all three bands mentioned. These are automatically operated radio stations that retransmit the radio signals they receive and so, as it were, amplify them. Since these repeaters are usually set up on mountains, radio towers or high-rise buildings, they increase the achievable ranges drastically, often to 50 km and more. This makes reliable communication possible over surprisingly long distances, even under difficult conditions. For example, a radio amateur in Ulm will hardly be able to reach an acquaintance in Munich with a handheld radio, even under the most favourable conditions – but if both use the 2 m amateur radio repeater DB0ZU on the Zugspitze, a conversation is easily possible. Furthermore, the repeaters are linked with each other via the internet. Via the digital DMR repeater network Brandmeister, even worldwide contacts are possible, since these repeaters exist all over the world. There are also a few repeaters for CB, Freenet and PMR in Germany; compared with amateur radio, with its almost 100% area coverage, however, this is a vanishingly small number.

Since Class N is currently a purely German matter, you may unfortunately transmit only from German soil. So a Class N radio amateur may talk without any problem from Saarbrücken with a station in Warsaw, for example on the 10 m band – but as soon as the same radio amateur in Frankfurt an der Oder has crossed the Polish border, they may no longer do so, because they are no longer on German territory.

In return, Class N is also comparatively easy to obtain. As regards the technical topics asked about, care was even taken that they match the subjects taught in years 8 and 9 of German schools, so that the Class N examination should be relatively easy for pupils to pass as well.

Class E

Compared with the entry-level Class N, Class E offers additional possibilities. The additional HF bands 160 m, 80 m and 15 m ensure that on almost every day of the year you can find the opportunity for long-distance contacts (from 500 km) for at least a few hours. The considerably higher transmit powers (75 W or 100 W PEP at the antenna input on most relevant bands, compared with 10 W ERP/EIRP in Class N) and the possibility of boosting these 75 W or 100 W further with a directional antenna allow considerably greater ranges and more reliable contacts. With a good antenna and good timing, daytime contacts on the 10 m or 15 m band to, for example, Australia or South America are nothing out of the ordinary. The 80 m band and the 160 m band, in turn, allow so-called NVIS contacts: through reflection of the signals at the ionosphere you can reach a contact partner, especially at night, even behind a mountain ridge that would normally make direct communication impossible.

The 13 cm (2.4 GHz) and 6 cm (5.7 GHz) bands lie close to the Wi-Fi frequency ranges and allow participation in HAMnet – a kind of wireless internet for radio amateurs, so to speak; however, because of the much higher transmit powers permitted compared with Wi-Fi (5 W PEP compared with 0.5 W EIRP), plus the possibility of using directional antennas for transmitting too, much greater ranges can be achieved here, in some cases over dozens of kilometres. The geostationary satellite QO-100 also uses the gigahertz frequency bands. Through it, contacts are possible from Brazil, Europe and Africa to India.

Class E is recognised in some other European countries, so that you may also operate there, for example on holiday. As of 01/2024 these are the following countries: Austria, Switzerland, Liechtenstein, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Denmark, the Czech Republic, Poland; also Albania, Australia, Belarus, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Estonia, Finland, Greenland, Iceland, Israel, Canada, Croatia, Latvia, Lithuania, Moldova, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Norway, Portugal, Romania, Russia, Slovakia, Slovenia, Hungary, Ukraine, the USA (no guarantee). The exact list can be found at the DARC foreign affairs department (DARC-Auslandsreferat).

Compared with Class N, the technical questions are of course considerably more demanding, but in our experience with the old system these too are manageable for people who did not exactly count mathematics and physics among their favourite subjects at school.

Class A

What first catches the eye are the power limits, which are considerably higher again than in Class E on almost all bands; for example, up to 750 W PEP is permitted on most HF bands. This also makes more unusual modes of propagation possible: with EME (Earth–Moon–Earth), our Moon, about 400,000 km away, is used as a reflector for the radio waves. Troposcatter, in turn, is a type of link for very high powers that was popular with the military in the past and allows very reliable contacts up to about 500 km on frequencies from the VHF range upwards.
Many radio amateurs, however, do not make use of these high power limits at all, but obtain Class A mainly to enjoy the additional, particularly attractive HF bands: of the HF bands 40 m, 30 m, 20 m and 17 m, at least one allows long-distance contacts at almost any time of day or night. Likewise, depending on the time of day and solar activity, the 60 m and even 40 m bands are sometimes better suited to NVIS contacts than 160 m and 80 m. In addition, the 630 m medium-wave band and the 2200 m long-wave band allow contacts over several hundred kilometres at practically any time of day.

In simplified terms, a German Class A amateur radio licence is recognised in many countries of the world – unlike Class E, in particular also in the popular holiday countries France, Italy, Spain, Bulgaria, Greece, Turkey, Great Britain and Sweden (no guarantee). In many countries you may even simply start operating with the German Class A without registering in advance; in other countries, with Class A you can apply for a call sign of the country concerned before your holiday, without having to take an amateur radio examination in that country.

Compared with Class E, the technical questions are more demanding again. In our experience with the old question catalogue, however, this examination too is manageable for people who have a certain basic understanding of mathematics and, above all, enjoy understanding scientific and physical relationships. Graduate electrical engineers will master the examination with a few days of preparation; people with less technical and mathematical training will need to acquire a little more knowledge accordingly. Please do not let the mathematics put you off: the "worst" things that appear in the formulas are logarithms, roots, powers and trigonometric functions. A great many radio amateurs with Class A call signs are not electrical engineers but, for example, lawyers, doctors, computer scientists or business people.

And now?

So which class is the right one for you? In other words, how much technical material should you learn? Fortunately, thanks to the new structure of the examinations, you can be bold here right from the start at relatively little risk and aim for Class E or even Class A. For suppose you register straight away for the Class A examination, but then pass only the parts on operating practice/regulations and the Class N technical part, and not the technical parts for Class E and A: in this case you would still be assigned a Class N licence for a start. You can then retake the examination parts for Class E or A at a later date and, once you have passed them, receive a Class E or Class A licence accordingly.
