The question is about a "mains lead", that is, about a
power supply at $\qty{230}{\volt}$ and $\qty{50}{\hertz}$.
The aim is to attenuate RF currents on the lead as much as
possible, but without impairing the power supply with a
$\qty{50}{\hertz}$ alternating current.
So what is wanted is a filter that strongly attenuates high frequencies
but lets low frequencies (here the $\qty{50}{\hertz}$) pass as
unhindered as possible: a low-pass filter.

If you do not recognise the low-pass filter circuit straight away,
you can also find the right circuit by a process of
elimination:

Capacitors have a large capacitive reactance at low frequencies
and a smaller capacitive reactance at high frequencies.
This rules out all the circuits in which
capacitors are connected in series with the supply conductors,
because these series capacitors would let the
RF currents pass but present a relatively high impedance to the
$\qty{50}{\hertz}$ power supply,
which is the exact opposite of the desired effect.
That leaves only the circuit in which an inductor is inserted in series
with each of the two cores of the supply lead and
the capacitors are connected between the two cores.

The series inductors have only a very small impedance
for the $\qty{50}{\hertz}$ AC voltage of the power supply,
so they hardly obstruct the power supply through the
mains lead. For the RF superimposed on the power supply, however,
they present a high inductive reactance.

The capacitors between the two cores more or less short-circuit
the superimposed RF between the two cores, since for the RF they have
a very small capacitive reactance. For the
$\qty{50}{\hertz}$ AC voltage of the power supply, however, their
capacitive reactance is so high that the current flowing through the
capacitors at $\qty{50}{\hertz}$ is negligible.
