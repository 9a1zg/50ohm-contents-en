# Explanation

The near field of an antenna is divided into a reactive near field and a radiating near field. The transition to the far field is gradual; there is no sharp boundary.

In the reactive near field (typically for distances $d < \frac{\lambda}{2\pi}$) the electromagnetic field components vary strongly with position. Here the electric field strength $E$ and the magnetic field strength $H$ have no fixed phase or amplitude relationship to each other. It is not permissible to apply far-field approximations in this region.

In the radiating near field (also called the Fresnel region, typically for $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi} \le d \lesssim 4\lambda$) the radiated wave already dominates. The structure of the field, however, still depends on distance and direction, so the far-field conditions are not fully met.

If the far-field approximation is nevertheless used in the radiating near field, in many cases it gives a conservative estimate of the field strength, i.e. the calculated values are higher than the field strengths that actually occur. As a rule, this gives a safe assessment. This statement does not hold in general, however, and in particular not for electrically small antennas or magnetically dominated radiators, where higher field strengths can occur locally in the near field than the far-field approximation predicts.
