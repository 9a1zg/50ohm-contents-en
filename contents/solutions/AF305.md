In the first stage (AF) an audio signal is produced. To convert this
audio signal to the intermediate frequency (IF), it is mixed in
the second stage – the balanced modulator – with the signal from the CO
(crystal oscillator).

In an ideal balanced modulator, the mixing process produces from
the two signals fed in (here AF and CO) only two new signals at the output:
the sum frequency and the difference frequency
of the two input signals. The sum frequency forms
the upper sideband (USB), the difference frequency forms the
lower sideband (LSB). As an analogue circuit, however, a
100% ideal balanced modulator cannot be built, so that in
practice, besides USB and LSB, a few other signals are also present
at the output (remnants of the two input signals at reduced
amplitude, and further mixing products).

An SSB transmitter is meant to transmit exactly the wanted sideband (with
a width of at most $\qty{2.7}{\kilo\hertz}$) and nothing else –
nothing below and nothing above it, i.e. at
the position of the question mark you need a band-pass filter with a bandwidth
of at most $\qty{2.7}{\kilo\hertz}$ and steep
skirts, which lets only the wanted sideband through. As
an analogue circuit, this can only be achieved with a crystal filter.

The other answers cannot be right, for several
reasons:

* A band-pass filter is needed, because everything above and
  everything below the wanted sideband must be filtered
  out. A low-pass or high-pass filter would only filter either
  above or below the wanted sideband.

* Apart from that, the steep skirts needed for a sideband filter
  could not be achieved with RC or RL networks;
  they can only be achieved with a crystal filter.

* A notch filter would make no sense here, because it is the
  exact opposite of a band-pass filter – a notch filter filters out a
  narrow range and lets everything else through.
